Imperial College London · in collaboration with LMU Munich

Cross-species comparative blood transcriptomics of human MIS-C and feline infectious peritonitis

Supervisors: Prof. Aubrey Cunnington and Dr Claire Dunican
Thesis grade: Distinction · Manuscript in preparation

Two severe inflammatory diseases are linked to coronavirus infection in two different species: MIS-C after infection, and FIP during ongoing infection. If their blood gene-expression responses agree, the cat disease can help explain the human one.

Why the design matters

Each species has its own cases and controls. I measured the disease effect inside each species first, then compared the two effects. This removes most of the difference that comes only from species.

The feline control group is small (n = 5), so I treat feline effect sizes with care.

I built the workflow in R. Select a step, or press ↓ from the Pipeline tab and then use ← →, to see what each step does.

Of 3,262 orthologue pairs significant in both species, 81.4% change in the same direction.

What the two diseases share, and where they differ

  • Shared Neutrophil-associated inflammatory programmes
  • Divergent Interferon-associated responses

Found with transcriptional-module and pathway analyses.

Blood is a mixture of cells

If a disease increases the number of neutrophils in blood, neutrophil genes go up even when no single cell changes its gene regulation. I examined this with CIBERSORTx cell-mixture estimates and feline clinical measurements.

What this means for the result

Part of the shared signal can come from changes in cell composition. So I report the concordance as a shared blood response, and I do not claim that it is all regulation inside cells.

01/2025 – 05/2025BPRI, Noida · Project lead

Huntingtin–HAP40 complex

Can a small molecule be identified that binds the huntingtin–HAP40 complex and offers a route to lowering mutant huntingtin?

Structure quality checksVirtual screeningToxicity and BBB prediction

Background and rationale

HAP40 binds huntingtin and stabilises it in a defined conformation. Elevated HAP40 promotes clearance of mutant huntingtin aggregates and improves motor and behavioural deficits in mouse models, whereas Hap40 knockdown exacerbates aggregation and neurodegeneration (1, 2). The interface between the two proteins is therefore a candidate site for small-molecule intervention.

Methodology

The workflow comprised six stages: structural data retrieval and validation; sequence alignment and domain analysis; structural characterisation and visualisation; structural and functional annotation; computer-aided drug design; and predicted toxicity assessment in neural tissue. Structure-quality checks were automated in a custom Biopython script.

Results

Structural validation. The complex (PDB 6X9O) satisfied quality criteria: 0.1% of residues occupied disallowed Ramachandran regions (VAL2250, HIS1918, SER309), and the RMSD against 8VLX was 0.48 Å. InterProScan identified three disordered regions, spanning residues 14–87, 419–484 and 1178–1227. The maximum B-factor, 95.44 Å², occurred at HIS1512 within an 'RYHSKQ' cassette of the N-terminal HEAT repeat. Cavity detection returned ten druggable pores with hydrophilic linings (radius 1.17–2.31 Å; length 33.1–352.6 Å).

Virtual screening and predicted toxicity. Blind docking in CB-Dock2, with each ligand then screened for predicted toxicity in neural tissue. Select a ligand to see its profile. All four were predicted to be blood–brain barrier permeant.

−8.8−9.5−10.2−10.9

Vina score, kcal/mol. Further right means stronger predicted binding. Amber marks a ligand with a predicted toxicity flag.

Weakest predicted binding of the four, 1.6 kcal/mol behind withanolide D — and the only ligand returned inactive for both neurotoxicity (p = 0.90) and cytotoxicity (p = 0.84). Lowest lipophilicity of the series (logP 1.25; TPSA 130.61 Å²). This is the one I took forward.

Interpretation

Epigallocatechin was prioritised despite returning the weakest docking score, 1.6 kcal/mol below withanolide D, because it was the only ligand predicted inactive for both neurotoxicity and cytotoxicity. For a central-nervous-system target requiring chronic administration, a 1.6 kcal/mol difference in predicted affinity is unlikely to outweigh a cytotoxicity probability of 0.98.

The flexible, charged cassette further provides an attachment point for an E3-ligase-recruiting motif, which would permit a PROTAC or molecular-glue strategy directed at degradation of mutant huntingtin rather than inhibition.

All values reported are computational predictions and require experimental validation.

07/2024 – 11/2024VIT, Vellore · Undergraduate thesis

Chaperone binding to Tau

Which chaperone binds Tau most strongly, and can that interface be reused as the basis for a Tau-targeting delivery protein?

Structural model assessmentProtein–protein dockingBinding affinity prediction

Background and rationale

Tau stabilises neuronal microtubules. Hyperphosphorylation promotes its aggregation into neurofibrillary tangles and disrupts axonal transport. Molecular chaperones bind unfolded substrates and suppress aggregation, and are therefore candidate therapeutic agents in Alzheimer disease.

Chaperones examined for this purpose are drawn from mammals and eubacteria — Hsp27, αB-crystallin, Hsp70, DNAJB6, clusterin and the BRICHOS domain — while archaeal chaperones remain untested. One chaperone was therefore selected from each domain of life, on the basis that each operates under a distinct constraint:

  • Human Hsp70 (PDB 1S3X) — the most abundant human chaperone, responsible for folding a substantial fraction of nascent protein, and dependent on ATP and co-chaperones.
  • E. coli Hsp33 (PDB 1VZY) — a redox-regulated holdase activated by oxidative stress, functioning independently of ATP under conditions in which ATP-dependent chaperones are inactivated.
  • Archaeal HypC (PDB 2Z1C) — from Thermococcus kodakaraensis, an organism growing at 85–100 °C, adapted to maintain protein structure under extreme conditions.

Methodology

The Tau model was obtained from AlphaFold (AF-P10636) and the chaperone structures from the PDB. For human Hsp70 the nucleotide-binding (ATPase) domain was used — PDB 1S3X, residues 1–382 at 1.84 Å — rather than the full-length protein, because the objective was an interaction surface resolved well enough to serve as a template for protein engineering. All starting structures were validated before docking by PROCHECK for stereochemistry and ERRAT2 across five regions of Tau, with Ramachandran analysis placing more than 90% of residues in favoured regions.

Structures were prepared in PyMOL by removal of water, ligands and extraneous chains and addition of hydrogen atoms; missing residues were modelled in SWISS-MODEL. Each chaperone was docked to Tau in ClusPro 2.0 under blind conditions, without assumption of the interface, and the ten energy-ranked models per pair were retained. Binding free energy, dissociation constant and interface contacts were estimated in PRODIGY.

Results

Predicted ΔG of the top-ranked model of ten. A longer bar denotes stronger predicted binding.

Predicted dissociation constants differed by five orders of magnitude: Kd 1.9 pM for Hsp33–Tau against 220 nM for Hsp70–Tau.

The difference was not confined to a single pose. Four of ten Hsp33 models returned ΔG at or below −12.8 kcal/mol (−16.0, −15.7, −15.2 and −13.7), whereas only one of ten Hsp70 models exceeded −7.0 kcal/mol, the remainder falling between −4.4 and −6.9 kcal/mol.

Interface composition also differed. In the top-ranked model of each pair, intermolecular contacts for Hsp33–Tau against Hsp70–Tau were: polar–polar 10 against 0; polar–apolar 41 against 7; charged–apolar 40 against 32; apolar–apolar 26 against 13; and charged–polar 9 against 3. Non-interacting surface was comparable between complexes (charged 25.2% against 24.4%; apolar 44.0% against 45.9%), indicating that the difference resides at the interface rather than in overall surface composition.

Interpretation

Hsp33 is predicted to bind Tau substantially more strongly than Hsp70. The ten polar–polar contacts formed by Hsp33, against none for Hsp70, are consistent with the properties of Tau, which is intrinsically disordered and polar across much of its sequence. The two chaperones operate under different constraints: Hsp70 engages a large and heterogeneous substrate pool and so trades maximal affinity for versatility and ATP-dependent release, whereas Hsp33 is subject to no equivalent requirement.

Hsp33 is activated by oxidative stress, a condition also present in the Alzheimer brain, so the trigger for its activity coincides with the environment in which Tau aggregates. This identifies Hsp33 as a template for the design of a synthetic binder reproducing both its interface composition and its redox-dependent activation.

The archaeal candidate was rejected on protein properties rather than on binding. The thermal stability that recommended HypC also means it would not be cleared after delivery: it would require manual tagging for degradation, with accumulation toxicity as the cost of failure. As a protein foreign to the human proteome it carries an additional immunogenicity risk. Hsp33 was therefore retained as the design template.

These results are docking predictions. Experimental validation by surface plasmon resonance or isothermal titration calorimetry, together with Tau aggregation assays, is required.

Output

Undergraduate thesis, VIT Vellore. Seminar presentation with co-author M. Bora. Best Paper Award (shared), One Health international conference, January 2026. Supervisor: Dr C. Sudandiradoss.

05/2023 – 03/2026VIT, Vellore · Review, second author

E3 ubiquitin ligases review

How can E3 ubiquitin ligases be translated from a mechanism of disease into a therapeutic target?

Literature synthesisScientific figure designResponding to peer review

Background and rationale

The ubiquitin–proteasome system eliminates short-lived, misfolded and damaged proteins. E3 ligases confer substrate selectivity on this process and therefore determine which proteins are degraded. Their dysregulation underlies cancer, neurodegeneration and immune dysfunction.

That same selectivity is therapeutically exploitable. PROTACs and molecular glues recruit an E3 ligase to degrade proteins refractory to conventional inhibition, since degradation requires only a binding site rather than a catalytic one. The structural and therapeutic literatures on E3 ligases had not previously been integrated in a single review.

Methodology

All sections of the review were written collaboratively, with approximately equal contribution between the three authors. I designed all five figures and prepared the responses to peer review across one revision cycle.

The review covers the four canonical families — RING, HECT, RBR and RCR — together with U-box ligases as a RING subclass lacking the zinc-coordinating domain, and the non-canonical families. Evidence is organised into three tables: family classification by catalytic mechanism and subfamily; disease association in cancer; and neurodevelopmental, neurological and genetic disorders. 93 references.

Results

Each family is mapped to its catalytic mechanism and to six processes under its control: proteostasis, DNA repair, development and differentiation, signal transduction, cell-cycle regulation and immune regulation. The regulation of the ligases themselves is then addressed through post-translational modification, signalling molecules, and autoinhibition and conformational change.

Three therapeutic strategies are compared. First, molecular glues and PROTACs, with clinical-stage examples: bavdegalutamide (ARV-110) in prostate cancer, vepdegestrant (ARV-471) in breast cancer and KY-474 in autoimmune disease. Second, E3-modulated CAR-T approaches, in which Cbl-b and Peli1 ubiquitinate ZAP70 and CD3ζ and so attenuate CAR T-cell signalling within the tumour microenvironment. Third, AI-assisted degrader design, exemplified by D16-M1P2, a generative-AI PROTAC that degrades PKMYT1 and is selectively lethal in CCNE1-amplified and FBXW7-mutated cancers.

Interpretation

Targeted protein degradation extends druggability to targets lacking a tractable pocket. The choice of E3 family is not a downstream chemical consideration: it constrains the tissue in which a degrader is active and the substrate range it can reach, and is therefore the primary design decision.

The CAR-T literature inverts this relationship. There the E3 ligase is the liability rather than the instrument, and inhibition of Cbl-b and Peli1 preserves receptor signalling that the tumour microenvironment otherwise degrades. The same enzyme class accordingly functions as a therapeutic tool in one strategy and as a therapeutic target in the other.

Output

Sriram S, Krishnakumar P, Sudandiradoss C (2026). Frontiers in Molecular Biosciences 13:1745654. Received 13 November 2025; accepted 13 February 2026; published 10 March 2026. doi:10.3389/fmolb.2026.1745654. Supervisor: Dr C. Sudandiradoss.

MSc research projectImperial · Dept. of Infectious Disease

PM2.5 and vape-liquid co-exposure

Does co-exposure to PM2.5 and vape liquid produce greater injury in alveolar epithelial cells than either exposure alone?

Cell culture and exposureRT-qPCRFlow cytometryAssay quality control

Background and rationale

Inhalation exposures rarely occur in isolation. Individuals relocating from rural areas to urban centres sustain continuous PM2.5 exposure from transport microenvironments, frequently alongside adoption of high-nicotine electronic nicotine delivery systems. Both hazards are individually characterised: each elevates IL-6 and IL-8 in airway epithelium through NF-κB and JAK-STAT signalling, and combined inhalation exposures have been reported to act co-operatively, producing impairment exceeding that of either hazard alone.

The stated hypothesis was therefore that co-exposure would exacerbate cellular distress, increasing cytotoxicity and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and shifting the profile towards programmed cell death, with an elevated Bax:Bcl-2 ratio.

Methodology

A549 alveolar epithelial cells were exposed for 24 h to PM2.5 at 20 µg/mL, high-nicotine vape liquid at 180 µg/mL, or both in combination.

  • Cell death — Annexin V/7-AAD staining acquired on a BD Accuri C6, with unstained and single-stained controls and compensation applied before gating.
  • Transcript abundance — TRIzol extraction, Agilent TapeStation quantification, and SYBR Green RT-qPCR on a QuantStudio 3 for IL-6, IL-8, Bax and Bcl-2, normalised to TBP.
  • Membrane integrity — CytoTox 96 LDH release, with lysis solution establishing maximum release.

Results

The hypothesis was not supported. Co-exposure was consistently less injurious than vape liquid alone.

Cell death. Vape liquid was the most cytotoxic single agent, reducing viability to 75.8% and raising necrosis to 23.5%, against 79.3% and 19.7% respectively in untreated controls. Co-exposure restored viability to 79.2% and necrosis to 20.5%, approximating control values. Early apoptosis remained between 0.0% and 0.1% across all conditions, indicating cell death by membrane rupture rather than by programmed pathways.

Transcript abundance (n = 1). Vape liquid alone induced a pronounced transcriptional response: IL-6 51.49-fold, IL-8 9.55-fold and Bax 19.16-fold, with a compensatory 5.73-fold increase in Bcl-2. Under co-exposure all three returned to baseline: IL-6 0.90-fold, IL-8 0.92-fold and Bax 1.08-fold.

Membrane integrity. The LDH assay returned values exceeding 100%, which cannot represent a valid cytotoxicity percentage. Chemical interference from vape components and incomplete lysis of the maximum-release control are the probable causes, so the assay was used for relative comparison only. On that basis the trend was consistent: highest release under vape liquid alone, reduced under co-exposure.

Interpretation

The two hazards are antagonistic. PM2.5 rescued the cells from vape liquid across viability, necrosis and every transcript measured. Two mechanisms are consistent with this result. Semi-volatile nicotine and propylene glycol may adsorb onto the high surface area of PM2.5 particles, masking reactive sites and reducing the effective delivered dose. Alternatively, PM2.5 is an established activator of the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant response and may prime that defence in advance of the vape insult.

One observation is not accounted for by either mechanism. Flow cytometry indicates necrotic cell death, while RT-qPCR indicates activation of apoptotic signalling. Necroptosis is intermediate between the two, and MLKL and RIPK3 are the appropriate markers for subsequent work.

The limitations are material. Transcript data derive from a single experiment normalised to one reference gene, so ACTB should be added. Transcript abundance does not establish protein level, and IL-6 and HO-1 require Western blot confirmation. The LDH assay requires an interference control and a more effective lysis reagent to return values within the 0–100% range. Submerged culture does not reproduce the airway, so an air–liquid interface model is indicated. The rescue effect itself should be tested directly by Nrf2 knockout.

Output

MRP scientific report, Department of Infectious Disease, Imperial College London.

10/2023 – 01/2024BPRI, Noida · First author

c-KIT kinase in GIST

Which cavity of the c-KIT kinase domain does sunitinib occupy, and what residues line it?

Binding-site analysisStructure validationMolecular dockingScientific writing

Background and rationale

Gastrointestinal stromal tumour is driven principally by gain-of-function mutations in KIT and PDGFRA, which render the kinase constitutively active. Imatinib is the first-line agent; resistance develops, and sunitinib is among the agents used subsequently. Rational design against resistant forms requires the binding site to be defined, which motivates a structural and docking characterisation of the kinase domain.

Methodology

Analysis was performed on the c-KIT kinase domain (PDB 6GQM), which carries AZD3229 as its co-crystallised ligand.

  • Validation — PROCHECK Ramachandran analysis prior to docking.
  • Cavity and interface analysis — PDBSum with SURFNET cleft detection; CATH for domain classification.
  • Docking — CB-Dock2 with AutoDock Vina scoring, performed blind across all detected cavities rather than at an assumed site.
  • Context — COBALT and BLASTP for alignment, STRING for interaction partners, PyMOL and RasMol for visualisation.

Results

Validation. Across 585 residues, 92.8% occupied the most favoured regions, 6.8% additionally allowed regions and 0.4% generously allowed regions, with no residue in a disallowed region.

Docking. Scores across five cavities were: C2 −9.8 kcal/mol (2,613 Å³), C3 −9.1 (1,689 Å³), C1 −8.8 (7,204 Å³), C5 −6.1 (384 Å³) and C4 −5.9 (486 Å³). The highest-scoring cavity was not the largest: C1 exceeds C2 by almost threefold in volume yet scores 1.0 kcal/mol lower.

Composition of the preferred cavity. C2 contacts 24 residues, extending from the glycine-rich loop (LEU595, GLY596) through the hinge region (GLU671, TYR672, CYS673) to the activation loop (ASP810, PHE811), and including THR670.

Structural context. The chain A–chain B interface comprises 3 salt bridges, 9 hydrogen bonds and 75 non-bonded contacts, between 20 and 17 residues respectively. STRING returned KIT ligand at 0.999 confidence and the GRB2 adaptor at 0.998.

Interpretation

Cavity volume is a poor predictor of ligand preference. Blind docking across all five cavities located the highest score in a mid-sized pocket rather than the largest, which supports exhaustive cavity scanning over selection of an assumed site.

The composition of that cavity is the informative result. THR670 is the gatekeeper residue of c-KIT, and ASP810–PHE811 constitutes the opening of the DFG motif. Both lie within the region in which imatinib resistance arises, so the cavity preferred by sunitinib coincides with the region altered by resistance mutations, consistent with its use as a second-line agent.

These are docking predictions derived from a single structure with one ligand. They define a cavity for subsequent design; they do not measure affinity, and no experimental binding assay was performed.

09/2023 – 10/2023Jaslok Hospital, Mumbai · Clinical laboratory intern

Transplant immunology laboratory

What must a transplant immunology laboratory establish before a transplant can proceed?

HLA typing by PCRCDC crossmatchDonor-specific antibody monitoringImmunoassay interpretation

Background and rationale

Before a transplant, the recipient's immune system has to be tested against the donor's tissue. Antibodies already present against donor HLA can cause rejection, so the laboratory work-up decides whether a donor and recipient are compatible at all.

Methodology

Worked within pre-transplant donor–recipient work-ups: HLA typing by PCR, complement-dependent cytotoxicity crossmatches, and donor-specific antibody monitoring. Interpreted immunoassay panels — ELISA, IFA, ECLIA and rapid diagnostic tests — against clinical reporting criteria.

Results

Contributed assay results to donor–recipient compatibility assessments during the placement.

Interpretation

This was the first laboratory I worked in where a result informed a clinical decision rather than a follow-up experiment. A transplant eligibility call cannot be repeated next week with a better control, so the reporting criteria, the controls and the borderline values carry a weight that a research assay does not. It changed how I treat a questionable result, and it is the reason I report excluded assays explicitly in my own work.

Output

Credit-bearing summer industrial internship, VIT course BBIT399J.
